"Auld Lang Syne" is a Scots-language phrase that translates literally to "Old Long Since," but its idiomatic meaning is "days gone by," "old times' sake," or "for the sake of old times." Here is a breakdown: 1. Literal Translation (Scots → English)
Auld = Old
Lang = Long
Syne = Since / Ago
Together:* "Old Long Since" → "Long, long ago." 2. The Famous Line Context
The phrase appears in the chorus of the poem/song:
"For auld lang syne, my dear,*
For auld lang syne.*
We’ll tak a cup o’ kindness yet,*
For auld lang syne."* Translation: "For the sake of old times, my dear / For the sake of old times / We’ll take a cup of kindness yet / For the sake of old times."* 3. Origin
Poet: Robert Burns (1759–1796), Scotland’s national poet.
Date: 1788.
Source: Burns didn't invent the phrase or the melody entirely. He wrote down an old folk song he heard from an "old man" and "took it down from his singing," adding his own verses to the traditional chorus. The phrase "auld lang syne" appeared in earlier Scottish poems (by Robert Ayton and Allan Ramsay) going back to the 1600s. 4. Cultural Meaning Today
While it is a general song about friendship, nostalgia, and remembering old acquaintances, it is globally synonymous with New Year's Eve (Hogmanay in Scotland).
The Tradition: At the stroke of midnight, people cross arms, join hands in a circle, and sing it to bid farewell to the old year and welcome the new one while honoring past friendships.
The "Cup of Kindness": Refers to sharing a drink (traditionally whisky) as a toast to those shared memories. Summary
When you sing "For auld lang syne," you are essentially saying: "Let's drink to the good old days and the friends we shared them with."
