**Soar** (originally **S**tate, **O**perator, **A**nd **R**esult) is a **general cognitive architecture** designed to create systems that exhibit intelligent behavior. It is one of the oldest, most mature, and most influential architectures in the field of Artificial Intelligence (AI) and Cognitive Science.

Developed initially by **John Laird, Allen Newell, and Paul Rosenbloom** at Carnegie Mellon University in the early 1980s, Soar was built explicitly to test **Newell’s "Unified Theories of Cognition" (UTC)** hypothesis: that a single computational structure can account for the full range of human cognitive capabilities.

---

### 1. Core Philosophy & Goals
*   **Unified Theory of Cognition:** Soar attempts to integrate distinct cognitive capabilities (reasoning, planning, learning, perception, motor control, language, emotion) into a single architectural framework, rather than building separate modules for each.
*   **Symbolic + Sub-symbolic:** While historically a **symbolic** (rule-based) architecture, modern Soar (versions 9+) integrates **sub-symbolic** mechanisms (Reinforcement Learning, Semantic/Episodic Memory) to handle tuning, bias, and experience.
*   **Real-time Performance:** Designed for agents that must react continuously in dynamic environments (robots, game NPCs, simulation entities).

---

### 2. The Fundamental Architecture: "Problem Spaces"
Soar models cognition as **search through problem spaces**.
*   **State:** The current representation of the world/agent (Working Memory).
*   **Operators:** Actions that transform one state into another.
*   **Goal:** A desired state (or class of states).
*   **Problem Solving:** Selecting and applying operators to move from the current state to the goal state.

> **Key Insight:** *Everything* in Soar—planning, reasoning, perception, motor control, even meta-cognition—is cast as search in a problem space.

---

### 3. The Decision Cycle (The "Heartbeat")
Soar operates in a continuous **decision cycle** (typically running at 10–50Hz for real-time agents). Each cycle has distinct phases:

#### Phase A: **Proposal (Parallel Rule Firing)**
*   **Production Rules (If-Then rules)** match against **Working Memory (WM)**.
*   *All* matching rules fire **in parallel**.
*   Rules propose **operators** (e.g., "Propose `move-forward`", "Propose `pick-up-block`").
*   Rules can also create **preferences** (numerical or symbolic) for operators (e.g., "Prefer `move-forward` > `turn-left`", "Require `check-battery`").

#### Phase B: **Decision Procedure (Serial Selection)**
*   The architecture examines the preferences for all proposed operators.
*   It resolves conflicts using a fixed hierarchy of preference types (e.g., `Require` > `Prohibit` > `Best` > `Better` > `Good` > `Indifferent`).
*   **One operator is selected** (or none, leading to an impasse).

#### Phase C: **Application (Parallel Rule Firing)**
*   Rules matching the *selected operator* fire in parallel.
*   They modify Working Memory: changing the state (effectors), creating sub-goals, or retrieving knowledge.
*   **Output commands** are sent to the environment (motor actions).
