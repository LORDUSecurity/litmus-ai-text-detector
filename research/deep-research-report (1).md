# Detecting AI-Generated Text: Techniques, Evaluation, and Best Practices

## Executive Summary  
The proliferation of large language models (LLMs) has made distinguishing AI-generated from human-written text both urgent and difficult. Major AI providers are now embedding **watermarks** – hidden statistical patterns – into generated text to aid provenance. For example, Google DeepMind’s *SynthID-Text* (a fixed-sampling watermark) alters token probabilities imperceptibly, and Anthropic’s Claude uses a similar SynthID-Text scheme with a private key. These watermarks allow detection *if* one has the key, but they cannot prove authorship or reliably identify small excerpts. In contrast, other detection strategies rely on analyzing the text itself: statistical measures (e.g. token log-likelihood or entropy) tend to be lower (more predictable) for AI text, and zero-shot methods like DetectGPT use the curvature of a model’s log-probabilities as a signal. Machine-learning classifiers fine-tuned on labeled examples often achieve high accuracy *in-domain* but generalize poorly across topics or models. Stylometric analyses examine linguistic cues (e.g. lack of lexical diversity or excessive repetitiveness) known to differ between human and AI writing. Finally, provenance checks (C2PA metadata, content credentials) and traditional watermark detectors (for models that provide a key) offer additional signals. 

This report analyzes these methods in depth, summarizing how current watermarks work, cataloging detection approaches (statistical, ML-based, linguistic, provenance, watermark-specific, forensic), and proposing an evaluation plan that stresses adversarial conditions (paraphrasing, translation, token edits, style transfer). We recommend a **multi-layered detection pipeline**: first check any embedded metadata or known watermarks (with provider keys), then compute statistical features and apply a calibrated classifier ensemble. Thresholds should be tuned to balance false positives/negatives and flag uncertain cases for human review. Robustness to evasion is crucial: for example, neural paraphrasing (PEGASUS, BART) can largely erase detectable watermarks, so the pipeline should monitor semantic consistency and require longer text for confident detection. 

No method is foolproof. Detectors can be biased (e.g. flagging non-native English text more often) and may give false confidence. Watermarks only indicate involvement of a specific model family (if signed by a provider), and their absence is not proof of human authorship. Ethical considerations (privacy, fair use, educational impact) and evolving regulations (EU AI Act) add complexity. We conclude with recommendations for practice and highlight open research needs (e.g. robust detection under paraphrasing, multilingual text, fine-grained watermarking, explainable detectors).

## 1. Watermarking and Patterning Methods  

Recent work has produced several **text watermarking schemes** that embed subtle signatures into AI-generated content at *decode time*.  The seminal approach (often called a *red-green* or *contextual hashing* watermark) was proposed by Kirchenbauer *et al.* (ICML 2023). In this scheme, the model’s vocabulary is split at each step into a “green” set and “red” set via a pseudorandom hash of the recent context. A small positive bias (δ) is added to the logits of green tokens so they are sampled more often.  Over a long passage, the fraction of green tokens exceeds the expected fraction, allowing a statistical detector (knowing the secret key/hash) to flag watermarked text.  This method is simple and produces no visible changes, but it **alters the model’s distribution** (so it is not distortion-free) and is sensitive to edits: changing or swapping even a few tokens can break the pattern.  

Google DeepMind’s **SynthID-Text** (Dathathri *et al.*, *Nature* 2024) refined this idea into a *fixed-sampling* watermark with minimal impact on output quality.  SynthID uses a tournament-sampling algorithm (via a secret key sequence) to subtly adjust the choice of next-token without directly boosting logits.  Practically, SynthID-Text adjusts each token’s selection probability according to a hidden statistical criterion.  In Google’s own words: each word is assigned a probability, and *“SynthID adjusts these probability scores to generate a watermark. It’s not noticeable to the human eye, and doesn’t affect quality of the output”*.  A key advantage is that the watermark can be checked later by scanning token probabilities with the known key.  Anthropic’s Claude now implements a similar scheme (“a version of the SynthID-Text approach”) for future models (post-August 2026), and is migrating older Claude models into this system.  Google’s Gemini also uses SynthID-Text for any text generated on its platforms. 

Other watermark proposals include *cache-based* or *linear reward* methods (e.g. adding signals in embedding space), but the main deployed approaches are these probability-pattern schemes.  The **strengths** of generation-time watermarking are that it can (a) in principle provide very low false positives (only text from the watermarked model yields the pattern), (b) survives some transformations (since it’s baked into token choice rather than metadata), and (c) can be combined with cryptographic metadata (see below).  However, there are significant **weaknesses and limits**: a watermark only works if the text is sufficiently long and is generated by the watermarked model. Very short outputs or cases where the model had almost no choice (e.g. quoting exact factual content) produce negligible signal.  Crucially, an adversary with a non-watermarked model can easily paraphrase or re-generate the text to erase the watermark.  Recent work shows that even weak paraphrasing can dramatically reduce DetectID scores.  Anthropic notes explicitly that their watermark detector *“can only answer whether a passage is **partly** written by Claude”* – it cannot confirm human authorship or identify any specific user. In practice, no key means the text is statistically indistinguishable from non-watermarked output.  Thus, watermarks provide evidence of AI involvement under ideal conditions, but **not a proof of misconduct or of human origin in their absence**. Table 1 compares key watermark schemes:

| Watermark Method        | Mechanism                                          | Strengths                                    | Weaknesses/Limitations                           |
|-------------------------|----------------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------|--------------------------------------------------|
| Red–Green Hash (KGW) | Pseudorandomly split vocab; bias “green” tokens | Simple to implement; high detection on long text | Alters distribution (quality trade-off); fragile to edits/synonym swaps |
| Fixed-Sampling (SynthID) | Tournament sampling using secret key bits    | Higher quality (almost no drift); resistant to small changes | Can be removed by paraphrase or re-sampling; requires provider key |
| Neural Paraphrase (Lexical) | Choose certain words/syntax via model (e.g. BERT) | Can encode info even post-generation | (Research stage) often degrades quality; breaks under large edits |
| None (Meta, OpenAI text)    | – (no standard watermark for ChatGPT text)    | –                                            | No embedded signal; detection must rely on external analysis |

*Table 1: Comparison of representative text watermarking schemes. Citations indicate original descriptions or implementations.*  

Major providers’ approaches reflect these methods.  Google/DeepMind has implemented SynthID (red/green or tournament) in Gemini; Anthropic is adopting the same for Claude.  OpenAI currently does *not* watermark ordinary ChatGPT text – its content-provenance efforts have focused on images and audio.  Meta similarly does not watermark text, though it watermarks images and video.  In summary, while watermarks are becoming a standard practice for closed-source APIs (and may soon be mandated by law, e.g. the EU AI Act), their detection requires access to the secret key or detector and fails on many adversarial edits. 

## 2. Detection Approaches  

Detection of AI-written text broadly falls into several categories.  Each has different inputs, requirements, and vulnerabilities (Table 2 summarizes the trade-offs):

- **Statistical Measures (Perplexity, GLTR, etc.)** – These “white-box” methods score how *surprising* the text is under a language model.  For example, one can compute the average log-likelihood or perplexity of the text under a known model: human text typically has lower log-prob (higher perplexity) than AI outputs, because LLMs produce more predictable text.  Tools like GLTR visually highlight the rank/likelihood of each word under a model. More recent work (DetectGPT) observes that AI-generated passages tend to occupy regions of *negative curvature* in the model’s log-probability landscape – by perturbing the text and measuring probability changes, DetectGPT achieves high AUROC in experiments (e.g. ~0.95 vs 0.81 for a strong baseline).  **Inputs:** text plus a reference language model (often the *same* or a comparable LLM).  **Accuracy:** These methods can flag differences in long passages, but performance varies (often moderate AUROC ~0.7–0.9 depending on length and model match).  **Robustness:** Poor under editing: paraphrasing, synonym swaps, or even just changing the prompt can yield very different perplexities.  For example, neural paraphrasing greatly increases perplexity and can fool such detectors.  **Cost:** Low–moderate (requires scoring text through a model, which is fast at inference).  **Typical errors:** False positives arise on unusual but human-like text (poetry, code, or very formal writing); false negatives when the AI text is crafted to be “low-perplexity” or the scorer uses a misaligned model.  

- **Machine-Learning Classifiers (Fine-Tuned Models)** – These methods treat detection as a supervised learning problem.  A pre-trained model (e.g. RoBERTa, T5) is fine-tuned on labeled human vs. AI text.  Early work (e.g. GPT-2 detector) achieved ~95% accuracy on its training distribution.  More recent detectors (GPTZero, OpenAI’s classifier) follow this approach.  **Inputs:** Text and a trained classifier; often requires a large dataset of both real and synthetic text.  **Accuracy:** Very high *in-domain* when test data resembles the training set (often >90%).  However, accuracy drops sharply on out-of-domain or cross-model data.  For instance, a classifier trained on news articles might mislabel creative prose or text from a different LLM.  **Robustness:** Generally weak against deliberate edits or adversarial paraphrases.  Attackers can fine-tune on model output or slightly rephrase to evade a fixed classifier.  **Cost:** High for training (large compute, careful dataset curation); moderate for inference.  **Errors:** False positives on legitimate human text that mimics AI features; false negatives on AI text that the model learned poorly or that is adversarially altered.  (Biases can also arise: e.g. detectors often misclassify non-native English writing as “AI”.)  

- **Linguistic and Stylistic Analysis (Forensic Stylometry)** – These methods extract interpretable features of writing style (vocabulary usage, grammar, punctuation, syntax patterns, coherence markers).  Studies have found AI text often has *less lexical diversity*, more repetitive phrasing, and different punctuation and part-of-speech patterns.  For example, Kumarage & Zubiaga (2021) categorize AI text weaknesses as (1) limited syntactic/lexical diversity, (2) unnatural repetitiveness, and (3) occasional coherence issues.  Tools like *Style Scalpel* visualize hundreds of such stylometric features for comparison.  **Inputs:** Raw text (no model required, though sometimes a reference corpora).  **Accuracy:** Useful signals on longer texts; published experiments show such features *can* discriminate with modest accuracy (often 70–85%) but are generally less powerful than ML classifiers.  **Robustness:** Often more robust to surface changes than pure statistical scores (since deep paraphrasing still may preserve some style cues), but strong paraphrases or translations can still mask these features.  **Cost:** Low (just extract counts, readability metrics, etc.).  **Errors:** Can be fooled by a human mimicking machine-like writing or vice versa.  Also, these features usually require enough text to measure reliably (the diversity metrics need paragraphs, not single sentences).  

- **Provenance and Metadata Checks** – These rely not on the text’s linguistic content but on external signals.  For example, images from AI providers often include C2PA Content Credentials (cryptographic metadata) that indicate if the content was AI-generated.  In theory, a similar system could tag text documents (e.g. PDF/XML metadata) with generation info.  **Inputs:** The content file (e.g. an image or document) with potential metadata tags.  **Accuracy:** If present and authenticated, this is foolproof – a verified digital signature directly identifies the source model/user.  **Robustness:** Very poor if metadata is stripped or absent (as often happens when copying text into a new document).  **Cost:** Very low (just inspect a header or use a verification API).  **Errors:** A missing tag means “unknown” rather than definitely human, and tags can be forged if the key is compromised.  OpenAI’s approach for images is to trust but verify via a portal, and explicitly *not* to conclude “human” if no provenance is found.  

- **Watermark Detection Algorithms** – If the text was watermarked by a known scheme (e.g. Google’s SynthID or Kirchenbauer’s red-green), detection requires that scheme’s **secret key**.  Using the key, one can statistically test whether the pattern is present (e.g. by checking the green/red token ratios).  **Inputs:** The text and the watermark key/algorithm.  **Accuracy:** High on long enough passages (often designed to yield >95% true positive at a low false-positive rate for sufficient text length).  **Robustness:** Limited. All watermark schemes studied so far can be disabled by paraphrasing, fine-tuning on an unwatermarked model, or even token shuffling.  In one analysis, Pegasus paraphrase attacks dropped watermark detection success from 100% to ~77% (Provable Watermarking) or worse.  **Cost:** Moderate – requires running the original model’s decoder or a reference LLM to recompute token distributions and apply the hash.  **Errors:** False negatives occur on short texts (insufficient signal) or edited texts. False positives are rare if the scheme is truly cryptographic, but theoretical attacks (learning the hash) could cause issues.  

- **Forensic and Source-Checking Methods** – These include plagiarism/fact-checking tools or cross-referencing the text with known corpora. For example, if a segment verbatim matches a news article or book, one might infer it was reused by an LLM (since LLMs often regurgitate training data).  Other forensic analyses look at metadata not meant for AI (like “last edited by” in a doc) or use stylometric authorship identification to see if the style shifts within the document.  **Inputs:** The text and external data sources (corpus search, known author fingerprints).  **Accuracy:** Highly variable and ad hoc. Can catch low-quality AI “copying”, but AI can also hallucinate original content.  **Robustness:** These methods are orthogonal to LLM tactics; they break if the content is novel.  **Cost:** Variable (web search is cheap; building stylometric profiles is moderate).  **Errors:** Very high false negatives (AI text can be entirely original) and potential false positives if coincidental matches occur.  

The following table provides a concise comparison of key detection approaches:

| **Detection Approach**         | **Required Inputs**                      | **Effectiveness**                             | **Robustness & Limits**                                         | **Typical FP/FN**                             |
|-------------------------------|------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|----------------------------------------------------------------|-----------------------------------------------|
| *Statistical (LLR, GLTR)*     | Text + access to a (same or reference) LLM | Moderate AUROC on long text (e.g. 0.7–0.9) | Easily evaded by paraphrasing or adversarial prompts. Struggles if model-probabilities are not known. | FP: very fluent human text; FN: polished or low-entropy AI output. |
| *Zero-shot Curvature (DetectGPT)* | Text + target model for scoring           | High AUROC (e.g. 0.95 vs 0.81 baseline) | Similar vulnerabilities as above (requires white-box access). | Same as above; may misfire if model estimates are inaccurate. |
| *ML Classifier (fine-tuned)*  | Text + pretrained classifier (trained on AI/human) | Very high *in-domain* (often >90%) | Fails on unseen domains/models; vulnerable to training-data shifts. Can be deceived by paraphrases. | FP: unusual human style; FN: atypical AI style or adversarially altered text. |
| *Stylometric Analysis*  | Text only (extract lexical/syntactic features) | Moderate (70–85% in studies) | Limited by text length (needs paragraphs) and clever human imitation. | FP: creative human text with simple style; FN: AI text with varied style or minimal content. |

| *Watermark Detection*         | Text + watermark key/algorithm (white-box) | Very high on long text (tunable threshold)    | Defeated by paraphrasing, re-generation, or decoding changes. | *Forensic Plagiarism Checks*  | Text + search/indexes of known content      | Patchy – can catch verbatim copies           | Useless for paraphrased or original content.                        | FP: common phrases appearing in corpora; FN: AI-original writing. |

*Table 2: Comparison of AI-text detection approaches. All methods can fail under adversarial edits or domain shifts, and often complement each other.*  

## 3. Empirical Evaluation Plan  

To rigorously assess detection methods, we propose a multi-stage evaluation covering diverse data and adversarial scenarios:

- **Datasets:** Use a mix of human-written and AI-generated corpora across genres.  Established benchmarks include parallel datasets where human text is paired with LLM outputs on the same prompt (e.g. *ArguGPT* for argumentative essays, *GPAbench*, *HC3*).  Also include “in-the-wild” collections (e.g. content scraped from forums with bot vs human labels, such as M4 or recent Reddit/Github postings). For training ML classifiers, reserve standard splits; for zero-shot/statistical tests, use held-out sets.  Ensure data diversity (news, social media, academic, creative writing) and multiple model sources (GPT-3/4, Claude, Llama, etc).

- **Baseline Metrics:** Evaluate under no-attack conditions. Metrics should include classification accuracy, precision/recall, and ROC-AUC for each detector. For watermarks, report true positive rate at fixed false positive rates (e.g. TPR@1%FPR), and vice versa. Include calibration curves since many zero-shot methods rely on thresholds. Also record execution time and resource cost (since some methods require model queries).

- **Adversarial Tests:** Following recent literature on watermark evasion, systematically apply transformations to AI-generated text and re-test detectors. Proposed attack scenarios include:
  1. **Neural Paraphrasing:** Use models like BART or Pegasus to paraphrase the text in-domain. Pegasus paraphrases are particularly effective at removing watermark signals. Check detector performance on paraphrased outputs.
  2. **Back-and-forth Translation:** Translate the text to another language and back (e.g. English→French→English) using MarianMT or similar. Evaluate the drop in detection scores.
  3. **Lexical Substitutions:** Randomly replace content words with synonyms (using WordNet or contextual embeddings) up to a certain ratio (e.g. 30%). Also test deletions of non-keywords and swapping of adjacent words.
  4. **Style Transfer:** Force the AI output into different styles (e.g. more formal, simpler, different dialect) using prompts or specialized models, to see if detectors still recognize AI patterns.
  5. **Compression/Abbreviation:** Remove stopwords or collapse phrases to mimic human edits, and assess watermark weakening.  
  For each scenario, measure how detection accuracy (and watermark score) degrades as a function of attack strength (e.g. paraphrase intensity).  This will identify which methods are most robust.

- **Human-in-the-Loop:** As a reality check, have human annotators attempt to distinguish a sample of texts (e.g. using tools like GLTR for hints), to establish a human baseline and identify easy/hard cases. The NRC survey noted that even experts often cannot reliably tell apart model vs human text, so detector performance should be compared against this baseline.

- **Adversarial Resistance:** If possible, evaluate “second-order” attacks: e.g. use one AI to paraphrase content from another AI, or fine-tune an open model on unwatermarked text and regenerate. Measure if detectors (especially ML classifiers) still flag the outputs. 

- **Evaluation of False Positives:** Include “null” cases: collections of pure human text (diverse authors, L1 vs L2 speakers) to measure false alarm rates. Some studies found detectors often mislabel non-native English writing as AI; explicitly quantify this.

By systematically varying model types, input length, and attack methods, this evaluation will quantify each detector’s **required inputs** (e.g. model access), **accuracy under clean and perturbed inputs**, **robustness to paraphrase/compression**, **computational cost**, and **failure modes** (FP vs FN).  For example, based on past work we expect that statistical detectors will drop to near-random on paraphrased text, whereas watermark detectors will have near-zero false positives (since human text has no secret key) but increasing false negatives as edits accumulate. 

## 4. Recommendations and Best Practices  

Based on current methods and adversarial insights, the following pipeline is recommended for AI-text detection (Figure 1):

```mermaid
flowchart TD
    A[Input Text] --> B{Check Provenance/Metadata}
    B -- Metadata Found --> C[Verify Signature; Label Source AI/Human]
    B -- No Metadata or Inconclusive --> D[Watermark Detection]
    D -- Watermark Detected --> E[Flag as AI-generated (with provider tag)]
    D -- No Watermark or NA --> F[Compute Statistical Features]
    F --> G{Run Classifier + Thresholds}
    G -- AI-Score ≥ Threshold --> E
    G -- AI-Score < Threshold --> H[Likely Human*]
    E --> I[Report AI (probability, confidence)]
    H --> J[Report as Human-Likely]
    I --> K[Optional: Human Review for Edge Cases]
    J --> K

    subgraph Note
        style Note fill:none,stroke:none
        K[ *”Human-Likely” means no strong AI signal detected. ]
    end
```

**Figure 1.** *Recommended multi-layered detection pipeline. Metadata/provenance checks (top) precede watermark and content-based analysis. Thresholds and human review help manage uncertain cases.* 

**Pipeline Steps:** First, if the content comes with embedded provenance (C2PA tags or provider metadata), use it to immediately classify or label the text. In the absence of reliable metadata, check for known *watermarks* (if any): for example, run the SynthID/Text detector with Google or Anthropic keys. A detected watermark is strong evidence of the *source model* (e.g. “Generated by Claude-Gemini family”). 

If no watermark is found (or none applies), proceed with content analysis. Compute statistical features (e.g. average log-likelihood, GLTR ranks) and feed them, along with stylometric features, into an ensemble of ML classifiers (e.g. a fine-tuned LLM ensemble and a logistic regression on hand-crafted features). The outputs should be calibrated: choose a threshold that limits false alarms to an acceptable level (for instance, prioritize <5% false positive rate for critical use cases). If the combined score exceeds threshold, label as “likely AI-generated” with confidence; otherwise label “likely human.” Always communicate uncertainty: the report should indicate that a non-detection is *not proof* of human origin (aligning with OpenAI’s cautionary stance). 

**Mitigating Evasion:** Since paraphrasing and editing readily break these signals, the pipeline should also flag cases where the text appears internally inconsistent or incoherent (a possible side effect of heavy paraphrasing). For example, a text that suddenly shifts register or changes style mid-way could suggest an adversarial edit; this warrants human inspection. Comparing parts of the text (first vs. later sentences) with separate detector runs can catch such anomalies. Including semantic checks (e.g. verifying factual claims or checking sourcing of quotes) can also catch AI “hallucinations.” In practice, we find that **multiple signals** greatly improve reliability: if at least two methods (e.g. watermark + classifier) agree, act on it; if conflicting, err on the side of human judgment. For instance, *“flag as AI”* only if (watermark detected) *OR* (classifier confidence is high *and* perplexity is low). 

**Thresholds and Calibration:** Set thresholds based on risk tolerance. In academic or legal contexts, even a small false positive can have serious consequences, so one might demand very high confidence or seek confirmation (text source checking) before action. Conversely, in mass content filtering (e.g. social media), slightly higher false positives may be acceptable to catch more AI content. Always monitor the false positive/negative balance: typical ML classifiers can be tuned (e.g. via ROC analysis) to trade off recall vs precision. 

**Comparative Table (Detection Pipeline Components):**

| **Stage**                 | **Purpose**                       | **Method**                        | **Inputs/Tools**      | **Notes** |
|---------------------------|-----------------------------------|-----------------------------------|-----------------------|-----------|
| Provenance Check          | Identify embedded metadata        | C2PA verification, content credentials | File metadata parser | Reliable if present, otherwise skip |
| Watermark Detection       | Detect provider-specific tag      | SynthID/Red-Green detector        | Text + watermark key  | High specificity; fails if no watermark or after edits |

| Verification / Review     | Human inspection of borderline    | Editor check, cross-reference, sense-checking | N/A                 | Essential for “uncertain” cases; consider educational/fair use context |

By layering these methods, one maximizes detection odds while minimizing reliance on any single signal. For example, even if a student runs their essay through a paraphrasing tool to break a watermark, statistical anomalies (lower perplexity) and stylistic uniformity may still hint at AI involvement. Conversely, a text that evades statistical detectors may still be caught by a watermark or provenance tag if it is genuinely from an AI API. 

## 5. Limitations, Ethical and Legal Considerations, Open Questions  

**Detection is Imperfect:** All methods can be fooled. Watermarks can be stripped (by re-generation or paraphrase), and statistical/ML detectors have non-trivial false negative rates on adversarial text. Critically, *absence of a watermark or flag does not prove human authorship*, as providers caution. Likewise, a “positive” AI flag should not be used as sole evidence of wrongdoing. For instance, an AI model could have simply edited human text (see the concept of AI “editing” vs generation), and a watermark might still appear.  

**Bias and Fairness:** Detectors often encode biases. Studies show that AI-detection classifiers tend to mis-label non-native English writing as AI-generated more frequently. This raises fairness issues: speakers of certain dialects or writing underrepresented styles could be unfairly targeted. Providers and practitioners must audit detectors for such biases. Transparency is important: as Canadian and EU guidelines emphasize, detection tools should be explainable and nondiscriminatory. (For example, if a classifier flags text because of unusual vocabulary, the user should have recourse to see why.)  

**Privacy:** Embedding watermarks does not encode personal data, but it does mark content as AI-related. Some users worry that hidden signals infringe privacy or autonomy. However, watermark keys are held by providers and cannot identify individual users; the watermark only ties text to *some* model output. On the flip side, scanning someone’s text for AI-signatures could be seen as surveillance. Organizations must balance the desire to detect fraud with respect for privacy.  

**Legal and Policy:** The EU’s forthcoming AI Act (Regulation 2024/1689) will *require* providers to label AI-generated content in machine-readable ways, essentially mandating watermarks or equivalent. Providers (as noted) will bear that obligation. Notably, removing a watermark post-generation is not currently illegal – Article 50 of the AI Act puts marking duty on the providers, not on downstream users. However, misuse of detection tools (e.g. accusing someone of misconduct without proof) could raise legal challenges. Ethically, educators like Ardito (2024) argue that detection tools should not punish legitimate AI-assisted learning; instead, pedagogy should adapt to a “human+AI” paradigm. These perspectives highlight that detection must be used judiciously, with clear disclosure to stakeholders.  

**Open Research Challenges:** Many questions remain. How can detectors generalize to new LLM architectures and domains without retraining? Can we design watermarks that survive more aggressive text transforms? How to reliably detect *mixed* content (part AI, part human) or subtle edits? Cross-lingual detection is underexplored.  There is also a need for **explainable detection**: rather than a binary flag, better tools could highlight *why* a text is suspected AI (e.g. “X% of your words were among the model’s top-1 predictions, a pattern typical of LLM output”).  Finally, an open question is the long-term arms race: as detectors improve, AI models may also be fine-tuned to mimic human statistical fingerprints (e.g. by increasing entropy) – and conversely, detectors will evolve new strategies (e.g. generative watermark removal). Interdisciplinary collaboration (AI, linguistics, law, education) is essential to address these challenges responsibly.
